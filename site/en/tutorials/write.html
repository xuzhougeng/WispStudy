Tutorial 14 · Write

Write a paper: candidate sentences, citations and sources

The data analysis is done and it is time to write the introduction of your own paper. This tutorial walks through the Writing page. First you write down what you mean in your own language. Then you see how the papers in the library write their introductions, add citations to your paragraphs, and check for each citation which sentence of the original it rests on. You can also give the assistant one instruction and let it write straight into the manuscript. At the end you write a table, check it in “Preview” as a reader will see it, and export to Word.

Scenario
Write the introduction for a paper on a single-nucleus atlas of the cotton root, with the library “Plant single-cell transcriptomics” right beside it.
Needs
A few papers with full text in the library; a model provider set up in “Settings › AI”.
Time
About 20 minutes, some of it waiting for the model.

1Create a manuscript and write down what you mean

Open “Writing” on the left of the library. The first time you come in, there is already an “Untitled manuscript”. Click the title above the manuscript to rename it; here it is called “A single-nucleus transcriptome atlas of the cotton root”. To start another one later, click “Manuscripts 1” at the bottom of the outline. Its menu has “New manuscript”, and “Import a Word / Markdown file…” to start from an existing draft.

On the left is the outline. A new manuscript is already divided into Abstract, Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion, and under each section name the outline shows how many words it holds (or “Not written yet”). Each section also has the part of a paper it belongs to (abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion). That part decides which part of the papers the candidate sentences on the right are taken from: in Introduction the panel says “Candidates · Introduction”. Both the name and the part can be changed in the section's menu under “Name and type…”, and “Add section” adds a new one. “English” above the manuscript is the language of the text. You can choose Chinese, German, French, Spanish or Japanese, and buttons such as “Translate to English” translate into it.

Beside the outline heading are two icons: the pen is “I write” and the star is “Assistant writes”. Start with “I write”. In Introduction, write three paragraphs: what single-cell sequencing does and how plant tissue is prepared for it, why cell type annotation in plants is still limited, and what this study did. You can write in any language; a fourth paragraph in Spanish comes in section 4. The text is saved as you write.

2See how others write this part

The “Candidates” on the right come from the same part of every full text in the library. Before you have written anything, it lists the openings of the introductions of the papers.

Sort the full texts first. A full text is split by its headings into introduction, methods, results and discussion. Some PDFs have paragraphs with no heading to go by, and the star button in Fig. 1 is for them. Click it and the model set for “Structure full text” under “Settings › AI › Tasks” sorts them paper by paper, and only the text of those papers is sent. This time all 12 papers were sorted within a few seconds: the progress bar read “Sections organized 12 / 12” and none failed. A paper that fails is listed with its message and the number on the button shows how many are left, so you can click again to retry. The sentences in introductions went from 1384 to 1379.

Find candidates for a paragraph. Put the cursor in the second paragraph and press ⌘ + ↩ (Ctrl + Enter on Windows), or click “Find sentences” below the manuscript. The model first turns the paragraph into several groups of English search words, listed below the search box as “Content”, “Argument” and “Phrasing”, for example:

Phrasing

protoplast isolation enzymatic digestion cell wall scRNA-seq nuclei single-nucleus snRNA-seq marker genes type annotation cluster identity reference atlas

Below are the similar sentences, each card giving the author, the year and the page. Move the mouse over a card and “Context”, “Copy” and “Original” appear: “Context” expands the sentences before and after, and “Original” opens it in the PDF. “All papers” under the search words narrows the range to one tag or to “★ Starred” papers. It appears once the library has tags or starred papers, so the demo library here does not show it.

Cite a sentence. The first card is the one expanded in Fig. 2, a sentence on page 2 of the introduction of a paper on Nepeta tenuifolia leaves. (You can also type your own words, for example nuclei isolation, into the search box and press Enter to look for a sentence yourself.)

Zhou et al., 2022

For plant cells, the existence of cell walls has hampered the acquisition of protoplasts, and the limited prior knowledge of cell identity has become a technical obstacle for plant scRNA-seq.

Put the cursor before the period of the first sentence of the second paragraph and click “Cite” on this card. A citation is inserted at the cursor, and this original sentence is recorded as the source of the citation. In the manuscript it shows as (Zhou et al., 2022) (Fig. 3); the text itself is [@Zhou2022]. While the cursor rests on the citation, a line below the manuscript gives the paper's title and this original sentence, and “Introduction · Page 2”.

Citations in the manuscript are shown in the citation format chosen in the “Export” menu: author–year is (Zhou et al., 2022), numbered is [1], numbered through the whole manuscript. Rest the pointer on one to see the paper and the original sentence it rests on. When the cursor moves into it, it expands to the raw text such as [@Zhou2022], which you can edit. The next figure is the Results section of the same manuscript. A candidate from the cotton protoplast paper was cited at the end of its one sentence, so it shows as (Zhang et al., 2023) (the raw text is [@Zhang2023b], with the b because the library holds another paper by a Zhang in 2023), and below the manuscript is the source in the same way.

NoteCandidates are other people's wording, to consult and cite, not to copy. Also check whose finding the sentence reports in the original. The sentence cited in Fig. 3 is from the introduction of that paper and states a background, not that paper's own result, and it says nothing about nuclei. A better basis for the manuscript's claim would be a paper that tested nuclei, in its abstract or results.

3Find citations for a paragraph

The first paragraph is written and has no citations yet. Select the whole paragraph and a row of buttons appears beside the selection, in three groups: “Find sentences” and “Find citations” only search the library and do not change your text; “Polish”, “Translate to English” and “Rewrite by example” give back changed text; the last is “Comments”. Click “Find citations”.

The model first turns each sentence into English search words, searches the records, abstracts and full texts of the library's papers, and then checks paper by paper which really support the sentence. Within a few seconds a card appears on the right: one group for each sentence, with the role of the sentence beside it (the first one is “Background”), and below it the ticked papers, each with the model's reason (here “Shared terms: single-cell, rna, sequencing”) and the paper's own sentence and where it is. The first sentence had two papers ticked:

Yao et al., 2026 · Original study

Here we employ single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial RNA sequencing to profile approximately 100,000 individual cells

The model ticked two papers for each of the three sentences, so the button at the bottom says “Insert 6 citations”. Untick a paper and the number drops: with one unticked the button reads “Insert 5 citations”. Sentences with nothing ticked get no citation, so look at what was found for them yourself. Click “Insert 6 citations”: the citations are added before the punctuation at the end of each sentence, as [@Yao2026; @Zhou2023] after the first, the text is unchanged, and ⌘ + Z undoes it. The stand-in model ticks by shared words only, so some picks are weak: the second sentence, about roots and leaves and flowers, got a trichome review and a paper on tobacco pollen. Read the original sentences before you keep a citation.

TipA paragraph written in another language finds English papers just the same; the search words are generated by the model set for “Translate”. The bottom of the card states the range searched.

4Polish, translate to English, rewrite by example

The manuscript is in English, so a paragraph you wrote in another language has to be translated sooner or later. Here a fourth paragraph was written in Spanish, with a citation typed into it. Select it and click “Translate to English”. The card on the right shows the original above (the words that change struck through) and the translation below, and under them “Replace original”, “Insert below”, “Copy” and “Discard”. Your text changes only after you click Replace, and after that it can be undone too. “Polish” and “Rewrite by example” work the same way, and the changed words are marked. Rewrite by example changes the structure and wording after the sentences in the library closest to the paragraph.

Compare before you replace: check that the terms follow the original and that the citation stays on the sentence it supported. In this run the translation comes from the stand-in model, which was given the English text beforehand, so it says nothing about how a real model translates.

5References and sources

Click the “References” tab on the right (the number on the tab is how many papers are cited). “This section” and “Manuscript” switch the range, and “Copy” and “Export BibTeX” are above the list. Under each paper is the source: the original text of the paper that the citing sentence rests on, with the part and page.

A citation typed by hand has no source. To try this, [@Zhang2023b; @Yamano2025] was typed by hand after the first sentence of the third paragraph. The first is the cotton protoplast paper, and the second is a study of lung damage in mice caused by mineral dust, cited wrongly on purpose. A star button with a 2 then appears above the list, meaning 2 citations have no source yet. Click it and the assistant searches each cited paper, and the model checks which sentence is the basis. This time it took a few seconds. Under Yamano 2025 a line in the warning color read:

References page

No support found in this paper: Here we build a single-nucleus atlas of the cotton root.

Click a source and the paper's PDF opens on the right, turned to that page with the sentence highlighted. Remove a source you do not want with the ×. Sources are kept only in the library; the text of the manuscript is always [@Key]. A source can also be a weak one: the sentence the stand-in recorded for the cotton paper is about cotton root, not about the claim, so judge each source yourself.

NoteNo warning color does not mean the citation is right. The model only checks whether the original has a sentence that says this. Whether the citation is apt, and whether the paper is the original study, you judge by reading the original.

6Let the assistant write

The introduction still lacks a paragraph: which species and organs the plant single-cell atlases in the library cover. A review-like paragraph that means going through many papers can be handed to the assistant. Click the star icon beside the outline heading to switch to “Assistant writes”. The middle becomes a conversation and the manuscript moves to the right. Type:

Instruction

After the first paragraph of Introduction add a paragraph on which species and organs the plant single-cell atlases in the library cover. Use only papers in the library and give citations. Write it in English.

The assistant read the section, looked up sentences in the library, and within seconds had written the paragraph into the manuscript. In the conversation it says only what it did:

Assistant

Inserted a paragraph after the first paragraph of Introduction. It names six atlases found in the library: cassava tuberous root, soybean root and nodule, Arabidopsis root explants, rice callus, woodland strawberry leaf and petal. Each citation carries the sentence it rests on. I did not look for cotton root atlases here.

  • The changes are listed one by one below the manuscript: click one to jump to it, “Undo” takes back that one, “Undo all” takes back all of them, and after “Keep” the marks disappear. A section changed just now has a dot in the outline.
  • Before the assistant wrote, the section's earlier text was kept as a version. The left arrow at “Version 3/3” above the manuscript turns back to an earlier one, and you can “Restore this version” (the text before restoring is also kept as a version) or go “Back to now”.
  • The sentences it took when citing were recorded as sources wherever they could be found in the paper, so the six citations of this paragraph can all be opened on the References page and checked.

In the new paragraph each citation stands right after the clause it supports, for example [@Song2022] and [@Cervantes-Perez2024] in the second sentence. Each claim rests on one abstract sentence of one paper, so the paragraph is only as good as those sentences: open the sources on the References page before you rely on it.

7Materials: put figures into the manuscript

The results part needs figures. On the Writing page, below the outline and above “References”, there is a row “Materials”. It opens to the pictures this manuscript can use: the ones you uploaded together with all the pictures kept in Figures (the pictures in Materials and in the Figures page under “Favorites” are the same set). The screenshots in this section use the Results section of the same manuscript, whose one sentence cites the cotton protoplast paper. Fig. 1 of that paper was kept in Figures first: it was cut from page 5 of its PDF and saved with “Save to figures”, which reads the label and caption from the page (for how it was kept, see Read one paper closely: read, mark, ask, note).

Click “Upload pictures” and pick a sketch of your experimental workflow that you drew, workflow.png. It appears first, marked “New” and “Uploaded by me”. Beside it is “Fig. 1” from Figures, saying which paper it comes from. At the top you can search captions, tags, notes, file names and the paper they belong to, or show only “Uploaded” or “From papers”.

Click “Fig. 1” and it goes into the section you are writing, on a paragraph of its own after the paragraph at the cursor. You see the figure right in the manuscript, with its caption under it. For a figure from a paper, the caption automatically ends with a citation of that paper. The caption is the figure's legend, which can be edited in the figure's own dialog (“Figures”); here the legend was shortened to one line, “Establishment of a method for cotton root protoplast isolation.”, because the one read from the page was long. You can also drag a picture file onto the manuscript or paste it into the manuscript, and it is taken into Materials and placed at the cursor.

In the text of the manuscript a figure is one line of Markdown, ![caption](figures/….png). You can see and edit it when the cursor moves to that line. Delete the line and the figure leaves the manuscript, while the picture stays in Materials.

8Write a table, preview it as exported

The methods have to say how the samples were staged, and a table says it most clearly. This section uses another demo manuscript, made on v0.18.0 in the same library “植物单细胞转录组”, with its title in English and its Methods saying which paper the stages of this study follow.

A table is written in the text with vertical bars: lines one after another, the cells separated by |, the first line the header, and the second a line of dashes between the header and the rest. Colons beside the dashes set how a column is aligned: |:--| left, |:-:| centred, |--:| right. A section can also have a small heading written with ###. Here the section is written like this (the citation inserted as in section 2):

The four stages and their counts of cells or nuclei are the numbers printed on Fig. 1b of that paper. While you edit, the table is these lines of text; the small heading is already shown as a heading, and the citation as (Wang et al., 2026).

Click the eye icon above the manuscript, “Preview” (its tooltip reads “Preview: the whole manuscript laid out, with its Markdown drawn”), and the whole manuscript is laid out as a reader will read it; click it again to go back to editing:

  • the table written with bars is drawn as a table, its header row shaded and the columns sharing the width by their content;
  • citations are shown in the format chosen in the “Export” menu, here author–year;
  • the entry under “References” at the end is the one the exported file has;
  • the ### heading is a step smaller than the section name Methods; an empty section reads “This section is empty.” and is left out of the export.

In the preview all three columns are left-aligned: the alignment written with colons is used only in the exported Word file. The table's number, “Table 1”, is an ordinary paragraph above the table, made bold with **.

9Export

Click “Export” above the manuscript. The menu has “Markdown (.md)”, “Word (.docx)” and “Copy manuscript”. When the manuscript has comments, the Word item reads “Word (.docx), with comments”. Sections with nothing written are not exported.

The lower half of the menu is the citation format in Word: “Citations in Word: author–year”, “Citations in Word: numbered [1]”, and the journal styles APA, Nature, Cell, NLM/Vancouver, IEEE, two GB/T 7714-2015 styles and Chicago, and last “Add a citation style file (Zotero’s .csl)…”. The selected item has a check mark, and the citations in the manuscript are shown in it too.

The exported Word file writes citations in the selected format and lists the references at the end. Exported from this manuscript, the text has citations such as (Zhang et al., 2023), the citation in the figure caption is the same, and at the end are the 14 papers cited under References, each as a full entry. A citation in the text is a Word field linked to its entry at the end. A figure placed in the manuscript is embedded in the Word file, the figure above and the caption below.

A table written in the manuscript is a real table in Word, not lines of text with bars. The manuscript of the section before, exported to Word and opened in LibreOffice: thin cell borders, the header row in bold, the second column centred and the third right-aligned, just as |:--|:-:|--:| says; the columns share the width by their content, as in the preview. The entry under References at the end is the same as in the preview. In the file the header row is set to repeat at the top of each page when the table runs over a page break; this table is short and fits on one page, so that does not show.

In Markdown the citations stay as raw text such as [@Zhang2023], and the pictures are copied to the figures folder beside the .md file. To typeset them in a proper citation format, switch to “Manuscript” on the References page, click “Export BibTeX”, and hand the .md, the .bib and figures together to Pandoc.

TipAfter your advisor comments in Word, how to import the file, handle the comments one by one and export a file with tracked changes is in the next tutorial.

Recap

  • Write down what you mean in your own language. Candidates show how others in the library write the same part, and “Cite” records one original sentence as a source.
  • “Find citations” finds citable papers sentence by sentence, with reasons and original sentences. It misses some, so look again yourself at sentences with nothing ticked.
  • The star button on the References page fills in the basis for citations that have no source, and marks those it cannot find in the warning color.
  • “Assistant writes” hands off the looking up of papers and the writing of whole paragraphs. Each change can be undone, and every section keeps versions.
  • In “Materials” you upload your own figures or find the ones kept in Figures, and one click puts them into the manuscript. In Word the figures are embedded in the file, and in Markdown they are in the figures folder beside it.
  • A table is written in the text with vertical bars, and the colons of its second line set how each column is aligned; “Preview” lays out the table, the citations and the references at the end as the export does.
  • Citations in the manuscript are shown in the format chosen in the “Export” menu, and the exported Word file writes citations in it, lists the references, and has written tables as real tables.
  • To go back and forth with co-authors on the draft, read on in Revise with co-authors: Word comments and tracked changes. To first build the frame of a review, go back to Write a review in stages.

About this tutorial. The screenshots were retaken in November 2026 on macOS with v0.18.0, with the English interface and a stand-in model that answers by fixed rules, so the numbers in this tutorial are those of that run. A real model answers differently each time, so the wording and numbers will differ when you follow along; the steps are the same. Section 8 (Figures 13 to 15) comes from a demo manuscript made in another run with v0.18.0, with no model involved; Figure 15 is the exported Word file opened in LibreOffice.

The assistant can be wrong. Its suggestions and answers need your confirmation; check key numbers and conclusions against the original.
