Tutorial 13 · Write

Write a paper: candidate sentences, citations and sources

The data analysis is done and it is time to write the introduction of your own paper. This tutorial walks through the Writing page. First you write down what you mean in Chinese. Then you see how the papers in the library write their introductions, add citations to your paragraphs, and check for each citation which sentence of the original it rests on. You can also give the assistant one instruction and let it write straight into the manuscript.

Scenario
Write the introduction for a paper on a single-nucleus atlas of the rice lamina joint, with the library “植物单细胞转录组” (plant single-cell transcriptomics) right beside it.
Needs
A few papers with full text in the library; a model provider set up in “Settings › AI”.
Time
About 20 minutes, of which about 8 minutes are waiting for the model.

1Create a manuscript and write down what you mean

Open “Writing” on the left of the library. The first time you come in, there is already an “Untitled manuscript”. Click the title above the manuscript to rename it; here it is called “Single-nucleus transcriptomic atlas of rice lamina joint development”. To start another one later, click “Manuscript 1” at the bottom of the outline. Its menu has “New manuscript”, and “Import a Word / Markdown file…” to start from an existing draft.

On the left is the outline. A new manuscript is already divided into Abstract, Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion, and under each section name is the part of a paper it belongs to (abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion). That part decides which part of the papers the candidate sentences on the right are taken from. Both the name and the part can be changed in the section's menu under “Name and type…”, and “Add section” adds a new one. “English” above the manuscript is the language of the text. You can choose Chinese, German, French, Spanish or Japanese, and buttons such as “Translate to English” translate into it.

Beside the outline heading are two icons: the pen is “I write” and the star is “Assistant writes”. Start with “I write”. In Introduction, write three paragraphs in Chinese: why single-nucleus sequencing is adopted more and more, what the maize ligule-region atlas found and what is still unclear about the rice lamina joint, and what this study did. The text is saved as you write.

2See how others write this part

The “Candidates” on the right come from the same part of every full text in the library. Before you have written anything, it lists the openings of the introductions of the papers.

Sort the full texts first. A full text is split by its headings into introduction, methods, results and discussion. Some PDFs have paragraphs with no heading to go by, and the star button in Fig. 1 is for them. On hover it says “Ask the model to sort 6 papers whose paragraphs have no heading”. Click it and the model set for “Structure full text” under “Settings › AI › Tasks” sorts them paper by paper, and only the text of those papers is sent. This time the 6 papers took under two and a half minutes. 2 of them showed “2 failed”, with the message “the model returned nothing”, and the number on the button became 2, so you can click again to retry. The sentences in introductions went from 548 to 526.

Find candidates for a paragraph. Put the cursor in the second paragraph and press ⌘ + ↩ (Ctrl + Enter on Windows), or click “Find sentences” below the manuscript. The model first turns the Chinese into several groups of English search words, listed below the search box as “Content”, “Argument” and “Phrasing”, for example:

Phrasing

leaf lamina joint rice leaf collar maize ligule brassinosteroids hormone response

Below are the similar sentences, each card giving the author, the year and the page. Move the mouse over a card and “Context”, “Copy” and “Original” appear: “Context” expands the sentences before and after, and “Original” opens it in the PDF. “All papers” under the search words narrows the range to one tag or to “★ Starred” papers: with the tag “玉米” (maize) the candidates shrank from 526 sentences to 101.

Cite a sentence. This time the maize ligule-region paper was not at the top. Type the English brassinosteroid adaxial hypodermal straight into the search box and press Enter, and it finds a sentence on page 2 of that paper's introduction:

Wang et al., 2026

Brassinosteroid (BR) promotes LA expansion by promoting ligular cell elongation in rice and other crops.

Put the cursor before the period of the second sentence and click “Cite” on this card. A citation is inserted at the cursor, and this original sentence is recorded as the source of the citation. In the text of the manuscript it is [@Wang2026]. In the version used for this screenshot (before v0.17.0), that is also what the manuscript shows (Fig. 3). While the cursor rests on the citation, a line below the manuscript gives the paper's title and this original sentence, and “Introduction · Page 2”.

From v0.17.0 on, citations in the manuscript are normally shown in the citation format chosen in the “Export” menu: author–year is (Wang et al., 2026), numbered is [1], numbered through the whole manuscript. Rest the pointer on one to see the paper and the original sentence it rests on. When the cursor moves into it, it expands to the raw text such as [@Wang2026], which you can edit. The next figure is a demo manuscript on v0.17.0 (see section 7). The citation shows as (Zhang et al., 2023), and below the manuscript is the source in the same way. The [@Key] in the other screenshots of this tutorial all look like this on v0.17.0.

NoteCandidates are other people's wording, to consult and cite, not to copy. Also check whose finding the sentence reports in the original. This sentence is in the introduction of that paper and states earlier work, while the sentence in the manuscript states that paper's own result. A better basis is in its abstract or results.

3Find citations for a paragraph

The first paragraph is written and has no citations yet. Select the whole paragraph and a row of buttons appears beside the selection, in three groups: “Find sentences” and “Find citations” only search the library and do not change your text; “Polish”, “Translate to English” and “Rewrite by example” give back changed text; the last is “Comments”. Click “Find citations”.

The model first turns each sentence into English search words, searches the records, abstracts and full texts of the 28 papers, and then checks paper by paper which really support the sentence. The first time it took under a minute and a card appeared on the right: one group for each sentence, with the role of the sentence beside it (this one is “Background”), and below it the ticked papers, each with the model's reason and the paper's own sentence and where it is. The first time, the first sentence had two reviews ticked:

Bawa et al., 2022 · Review

The review states explicitly that it can characterize common and rare plant cell types and states

This time the button at the bottom said “Insert 5 citations”. In this example we discarded the card and ran it again. The second time only Bawa 2022 was ticked, with the note “2 more sentences have no fitting paper”. But the third sentence (protoplasts, fresh nuclei and frozen nuclei are comparable as three inputs) is exactly the result of “Integrated experimental and computational workflows for single-cell transcriptomics in plants” in the library, and the model did not pick it. So for sentences with nothing ticked, click “Show similar papers” and look again yourself. Click “Insert 1 citations”: [@Bawa2022] is added before the punctuation at the end of the first sentence, the text is unchanged, and ⌘ + Z undoes it.

TipA paragraph written in Chinese finds English papers just the same; the search words are generated by the model set for “Translate”. The bottom of the card states the range searched, here “7 of them have no full text and were searched by record and abstract only”.

4Polish, translate to English, rewrite by example

The manuscript is in English, so the Chinese paragraphs have to be translated sooner or later. Select the second paragraph and click “Translate to English”. The card on the right shows the original above and the translation below, and under them “Replace original”, “Insert below”, “Copy” and “Discard”. Your text changes only after you click Replace, and after that it can be undone too. “Polish” and “Rewrite by example” work the same way, and the changed words are marked. Rewrite by example changes the structure and wording after the sentences in the library closest to the paragraph.

Compare before you replace: the translation wrote the original's adaxial hypodermal cells as adaxial subepidermal layer, and a space is missing before the citation. Terms follow the original.

5References and sources

Click the “References” tab on the right (the number on the tab is how many papers are cited). “This section” and “Manuscript” switch the range, and “Copy” and “Export BibTeX” are above the list. Under each paper is the source: the original text of the paper that the citing sentence rests on, with the part and page.

A citation typed by hand has no source. To try this, we typed [@Qin2025; @Chau2025] by hand after the first sentence of the third paragraph. The second is about marker genes in non-model species, and the first is a circadian rhythm study in Arabidopsis, cited wrongly on purpose. A star button with a 2 then appears above the list, meaning 2 citations have no source yet. Click it and the assistant searches each cited paper, and the model checks which sentence is the basis. This time it took under a minute. Under Chau 2025 it recorded a sentence from its abstract. Under Qin 2025 a line in the warning color read:

References page

No support for this sentence was found in this paper: non-model plants lack known cell-type marker genes, which limits the accuracy of cell-type annotation.

Click a source and the paper's PDF opens on the right, turned to that page with the sentence highlighted. Remove a source you do not want with the ×. Sources are kept only in the library; the text of the manuscript is always [@Key].

NoteNo warning color does not mean the citation is right. The model only checks whether the original has a sentence that says this. Whether the citation is apt, and whether the paper is the original study, you judge by reading the original.

6Let the assistant write

The introduction still lacks a paragraph: which species and organs the existing plant single-cell atlases cover. A review-like paragraph that means going through many papers can be handed to the assistant. Click the star icon beside the outline heading to switch to “Assistant writes”. The middle becomes a conversation and the manuscript moves to the right. Type:

Instruction

After the first paragraph of Introduction, add a paragraph: which species and organs the existing plant single-cell and single-nucleus atlases cover. Use only papers in the library and give citations. Write it in English.

The assistant read the manuscript, looked up candidates in the library, and in under two and a half minutes had written it into the manuscript. In the conversation it says only what it did:

Assistant

Inserted an English paragraph after the first paragraph of Introduction, outlining the representative species and organs of the atlases in the library: Arabidopsis root tip and young leaf; rice root cells, leaf sheath, caryopsis and node; maize root, leaf and ligule region; and sweet potato root tip, catnip leaf and Chinese cabbage shoot tip.

  • The changes are listed one by one below the manuscript: click one to jump to it, “Undo” takes back that one, “Undo all” takes back all of them, and after “Keep” the marks disappear. A section changed just now has a dot in the outline.
  • Before the assistant wrote, the section's earlier text was kept as a version. The left arrow at “Version 6/6” above the manuscript turns back to an earlier one, and you can “Restore this version” (the text before restoring is also kept as a version) or go “Back to now”.
  • The sentences it took when citing were recorded as sources wherever they could be found in the paper, so the 11 citations of this paragraph can all be opened on the References page and checked.

In the new paragraph the citations after each sentence are written separately, for example [@Liu2024] [@TenorioBerrio2025], and can be merged before submission. The [@Wang2026c] cited for “Maize datasets encompass roots and leaves” is a paper comparing sample-preparation methods, not an atlas, so places like this still need a look at the source.

7Materials: put figures into the manuscript

The results part needs figures. On the Writing page, below the outline and above “References”, there is a row “Materials”. It opens to the pictures this manuscript can use: the ones you uploaded together with all the pictures kept in Figures (the pictures in Materials and in the Figures page under “Favorites” are the same set). The screenshots in this section use a different demo manuscript: a demo library on v0.17.0 with one full text, whose Results part has a sentence about lateral roots of the lbd16 mutant, citing that paper. Its Fig. 1 is already in Figures (for how it was kept, see Read one paper closely: read, mark, ask, note).

Click “Upload pictures” and pick a sketch of your experimental workflow that you drew, workflow.png. It appears first, marked “New” and “Uploaded by me”. Beside it is “Fig. 1” from Figures, saying which paper it comes from. At the top you can search captions, tags, notes, file names and the paper they belong to, or show only “Uploaded” or “From papers”.

Click “Fig. 1” and it goes into the section you are writing, on a paragraph of its own after the paragraph at the cursor. You see the figure right in the manuscript, with its caption under it. For a figure from a paper, the caption automatically ends with a citation of that paper. You can also drag a picture file onto the manuscript or paste it into the manuscript, and it is taken into Materials and placed at the cursor.

In the text of the manuscript a figure is one line of Markdown, ![caption](figures/….png). You can see and edit it when the cursor moves to that line. Delete the line and the figure leaves the manuscript, while the picture stays in Materials.

8Export

Click “Export” above the manuscript. The menu has “Markdown (.md)”, “Word (.docx)” and “Copy manuscript”. When the manuscript has comments, the Word item reads “Word (.docx), with comments”. Sections with nothing written are not exported.

The lower half of the menu is the citation format in Word: “Citations in Word: author–year”, “Citations in Word: numbered [1]”, and the journal styles APA, Nature, Cell, NLM/Vancouver, IEEE, two GB/T 7714-2015 styles and Chicago, and last “Add a citation style file (Zotero’s .csl)…”. The selected item has a check mark, and the citations in the manuscript are shown in it too.

The exported Word file writes citations in the selected format and lists the references at the end. Exported from the demo manuscript of section 7, the text has (Zhang et al., 2023), the citation in the figure caption is the same, and at the end is one full entry under References. A citation in the text is a Word field linked to its entry at the end. A figure placed in the manuscript is embedded in the Word file, the figure above and the caption below.

In Markdown the citations stay as raw text such as [@Zhang2023], and the pictures are copied to the figures folder beside the .md file. To typeset them in a proper citation format, switch to “Manuscript” on the References page, click “Export BibTeX”, and hand the .md, the .bib and figures together to Pandoc.

TipAfter your advisor comments in Word, how to import the file, handle the comments one by one and export a file with tracked changes is in the next tutorial.

Recap

  • Write down what you mean in Chinese. Candidates show how others in the library write the same part, and “Cite” records one original sentence as a source.
  • “Find citations” finds citable papers sentence by sentence, with reasons and original sentences. It misses some, so look again yourself at sentences with nothing ticked.
  • The star button on the References page fills in the basis for citations that have no source, and marks those it cannot find in the warning color.
  • “Assistant writes” hands off the looking up of papers and the writing of whole paragraphs. Each change can be undone, and every section keeps versions.
  • In “Materials” you upload your own figures or find the ones kept in Figures, and one click puts them into the manuscript. In Word the figures are embedded in the file, and in Markdown they are in the figures folder beside it.
  • Citations in the manuscript are shown in the format chosen in the “Export” menu, and the exported Word file writes citations in it and lists the references.
  • To go back and forth with co-authors on the draft, read on in Revise with co-authors: Word comments and tracked changes. To first build the frame of a review, go back to Write a review in stages.
