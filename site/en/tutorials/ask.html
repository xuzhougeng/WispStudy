Tutorial 10 · Read

Ask the whole library, and check the answers

You meet your advisor tomorrow and want a feel for the whole field first: which species and organs are covered, what the protoplast and nucleus routes each cost, and where the papers disagree. In this tutorial the assistant reads the whole library to answer, and then you check the passages it cites one by one instead of taking its word. What the library cannot answer, such as what a tool's documentation says today, it can look up on the web; what you will explain to others, it can draw as a diagram.

Scenario
Before a lab meeting, an overview of the 90 papers in the “Plant single-cell transcriptomics” library.
Needs
A screened library in which most papers have a stored PDF; a model provider. For web search, a key for a search service (DeepSeek's can be shared with its models).
Time
About 10 minutes, most of it waiting for the answer.

1Set the scope first

Click “Assistant” on the left. Before a conversation starts, the page lists a few kinds of things it can do: “Find papers”, “Read papers”, “Organise” and “Write”. Under each group are a few ready-made sentences and slash commands; click one to send it. The line under the title is the scope: “Scope: 90 papers in the current list”.

The scope is the list currently shown in “All papers”. Search or filter there first, and the assistant reads only that part. You can also click “Papers” at the top right of the Assistant page and type words in the box at the top of the panel that opens to narrow it down: look for protoplast (or nuclei) and the panel lists only the papers that match; Figure 2 shows it. Each row of the panel has a plus sign on the right; click it (or drag the row into the conversation) to read only that one paper, and a line above the question box says so.

Here you want an overview of the whole library, so do not filter: use all 90 papers.

2Ask, and see what it read

Write the question in the question box and press Enter to send it (Shift + Enter for a new line):

Ask

What do the papers report about protoplast isolation and about how many cells or nuclei were sequenced? Please quote page numbers and original sentences from the full text.

After you send it, the scope line changes to “Provided records for 90 papers, 37 relevant full-text pages”. When there are too many papers to fit, the rest get only their titles and this line says so. Here all 90 papers were given as full records. Click “Papers” again and the panel now lists the materials handed to the model this time: each paper has a number P1, P2 and so on, then “Full record” or, for some, “Full record · 4 full-text pages”, and whether the paper has a full text.

This panel is worth a look: papers such as P34 and P52 are marked “No full text”, so anything said about them can only come from the abstract. The papers that do have a full text were sent only the pages relevant to the question, and when the assistant thinks that is not enough it searches and pages through them itself.

This time the assistant first searched the stored full texts, then read the pages around the hits (a gray row of tool calls marks each step), and then wrote six short claims, each followed by a source. Remember that the assistant in this run is a stand-in script that answers by fixed rules, so the wording is plain; the mechanics are the ones a real model goes through. The opening paragraph is:

3Read the sources and the check marks

Sources in an answer are written in two ways. [P13] says only which paper the claim rests on; [P33 p.2 "Thus, the prerequisite for applying scRNA-seq…"] also gives the PDF page number and the sentence word for word. Below the answer there is one card for each source: this answer has six. When there are more than 8, the first 8 are shown and the rest are kept behind a button, visible only when you open it.

A card with a quoted sentence ends with a check result: the app compares the sentence letter by letter with the text saved for that paper (only letters and digits count; line breaks and hyphens do not). The possible results are:

  • “Exact quote found”: the sentence is on the page named (or in the abstract).
  • “Quote found on p.N”: the words are in the paper, but on another page; clicking the card goes to the page where they really are.
  • “Only the first N% matches”: the beginning matches, and the rest was changed or spliced together.
  • “Quote absent from source”: it cannot be found anywhere in the full text.
  • “No full text to verify”: the paper has no readable full text, and the sentence is not in the abstract either.

This time all six quoted sentences read “Exact quote found”, and no other result appeared. But that only shows that “the sentence is in the original”, not that it supports the conclusion before it. Step 5 shows how to check that.

4Go back to the page of the original

Click a card with a quoted sentence, for example “P33 p.1 · The highest yield …”. The PDF of that paper opens on the right, turned to page 1, with the sentence painted yellow.

In P33 this sentence is from the abstract, where the authors report their own protocol with cotton taproots grown for 72 h; a sentence from an introduction that sums up earlier work would weigh differently. A matching program cannot see that difference; you have to read the context.

5Have the assistant check each claim

In the same conversation, type /verify and send it; with nothing else written, it checks the previous answer. It asks the assistant to list the claims one by one, find the source of each in the full text, read the context of that page, and write the claim, an excerpt from the original and how far it is supported. Where only the abstract is available, it says so and gives no page number.

The conversation first shows a gray row of tool calls (here five page reads, one for each page behind a quoted sentence). Click it to expand it: one row per call, saying which paper was read and from which page. A real model with a larger answer to check can take minutes, since it searches and reads much more.

The result lists the six claims, each with its excerpt from the original, and the excerpts all read “Exact quote found” as well. What is worth reading is how it grades each one. For the wheat claim it says:

Assistant

The sentence says about 15,000 nuclei were loaded, not how many were kept after quality control, so it is not a count of nuclei sequenced.

It marks claims 3, 4 and 6 as only partly supported and the other three as directly supported. These grades come from the stand-in script's fixed rules; with a real model they come from its reading, which is why you still open the original.

To keep asking about one sentence, drag across it; when you let go, “Quote and ask” and “Copy” appear next to the selection. Click “Quote and ask” and the sentence goes into the question box as a quoted block; write your question after it and send.

TipThe verdicts of /verify come from the model too. Where it says “directly supported”, still open the original and look, above all for numbers that will go into lab-meeting slides.

6Compare, search and keep asking

Click “New chat” to start another question. Two slash commands come in handy when preparing for a lab meeting:

  • /compare P33 P32: it reads the records and full texts of the two papers, compares research question, materials and methods, conclusions and limitations in a table, then says where they agree, complement each other or contradict each other. The labels depend on the scope: use the P numbers the panel shows.
  • /search ambient RNA: find a phrase in the stored full texts and records. It lists the papers that contain it with a page number and the original sentence, and says which papers have no stored text and were checked by record only, so nothing can be concluded about whether their text mentions it.

Every conversation is saved in this library. Click “History” at the top right to list past conversations and how many questions each had; open one and under the title it says “Past conversation: continuing will provide materials from the library's current content”, and you can keep asking.

When the assistant wants to change the library, it stops and asks you first. Ask it, for example, to “find open full text for papers that have no PDF yet”, and a card appears in the conversation that names the operation to be done and offers “Allow”, “Allow for this conversation” and “Deny”.

Questions you ask often can be saved as your own commands: in “Settings › AI › Commands”, click “Add command” and fill in a name, a description and a template; where the template has {}, whatever follows the command is put in. After that, typing / in the question box lets you pick it. A command is only a piece of prompt: it gives the assistant no extra tools and skips no confirmation.

NoteWhat is sent to the model provider you set up in settings when you ask is the question itself, the records and notes of the papers in scope, the relevant full-text pages, and whatever the assistant reads with its tools. For papers with no stored PDF the assistant can read only the abstract; the answer says so and gives no page number. For the few papers you will present at the lab meeting, drag their PDFs into the window to attach them, then ask again.

7Look beyond the library: web search

Some questions the library cannot answer: what a piece of software's documentation says now, or how a tool is meant to be used today. For these the assistant can search the web. First look at the “Web search” group in “Settings › Network”: “Search engine” offers “Auto”, “DeepSeek”, “Jina”, “You.com” and “Off”. “Auto” uses the first engine that has a key; with “Off”, the assistant does not search the web.

This run uses “DeepSeek”, and the label beside it reads “Using DeepSeek”. The DeepSeek key “Shares the key of DeepSeek's models”: left blank, it takes the key of the DeepSeek service on the AI page or the environment variable DEEPSEEK_API_KEY, so there is no second key to get. Keys for Jina and You.com come from their own sites.

Back on “Assistant”, click “New chat” and ask something the library has no answer to, but that someone working in this field would really ask:

Ask

Our samples are single-nucleus data from the maize ligule. Which tool or method do the current official docs of Scanpy and Seurat each recommend for removing doublets in snRNA-seq? Go by their current documentation and give the URLs.

The scope line reads “Provided records for 40 papers, 17 relevant full-text pages” (the library has grown to 40 papers by this run). The answer starts with a gray row, “搜索网页 ×4”, folded like the tool calls of step 5. In this build the rows of the tool calls are not translated yet, so they are in Chinese even with the English interface: 搜索网页 is “Search the web”, and 个网页 is “web pages”. Click the row and it opens into one row per search: the words searched and how many pages the engine sent back, for example “搜索网页：Scanpy documentation doublet detection scanpy.pp.scrublet · count 6” and “DeepSeek：6 个网页” (six pages). Two of the four searches were narrowed to one site, site:satijalab.org and site:scanpy.readthedocs.io.

The answer was complete less than two minutes after it was sent. It opens by saying where it comes from:

Assistant

This comes from search-result snippets. I didn't open the full pages, and none of this is from your literature library.

The Scanpy section names Scrublet (scanpy.pp.scrublet) as the one doublet method in the Preprocessing API and links the function page. The Seurat section finds only HTODemux and MULTIseqDemux, which need hashed or multiplexed nuclei, calls a GitHub discussion that mentions scDblFinder “a user thread, not official guidance”, and adds that it cannot prove a page does not exist from a search that did not find one. The last section goes back to the library: the maize ligule atlas in it (P40) removed doublets with DoubletFinder (v2.0.6) at a 1% expected doublet rate and then used SoupX, with a quote from page 10 of P40.

Note that the two kinds of source are not treated alike. A quote from a paper is compared word for word and the result is written on its card (step 3): here the one from P40 reads “Exact quote found”. A web page is only a link in the text, with no card and no such check. Before it goes into slides or a paper, open the link and read it yourself.

NoteWhat goes to DeepSeek when the assistant searches are the search words it writes (the lines in Figure 8), not your whole question or your library; the snippets that come back are then passed to the model you set up. Web search is billed at the prices of the service chosen, and one answer can search several times (four times here). To keep the assistant off the web, choose “Off” in “Settings › Network”.

8Have the assistant draw a diagram

An answer can hold a diagram. Click “New chat” and ask for the maize ligule study in the library as a flow:

Ask

Please draw a diagram: the main steps of the maize ligule single-nucleus atlas paper from sampling and library sequencing to its conclusions as one flow from left to right.

This time the assistant called no tool; it worked from the full-text pages sent with the question. The answer opens with the diagram: from “Sampling” on the left, through the libraries, cleanup, clustering, pseudotime and the hormone experiments, splitting into an early and a late stage and joining again at “Conclusion” on the right, ten boxes in all. Below the diagram the steps are listed again with their sources, and the cards below them read “Exact quote found” for all seven quotes. It was done within a minute and a half.

The assistant writes the diagram in Mermaid, and the window draws it. Move the pointer over it and three buttons appear at its top right: a magnifier, two squares and angle brackets. Rest on a button to see its name: “Actual size”, “Copy image”, “Show source”.

This flow runs across ten boxes, far wider than the column, and its words come out tiny. Click “Actual size”: the diagram is shown at its own size with a horizontal scrollbar under it, so you can move along it. The magnifier turns green; click it again (it is now “Fit width”) to fit the column once more.

“Copy image” copies the diagram as a PNG on white, to paste into slides or a manuscript. “Show source” replaces the diagram with the Mermaid source the assistant wrote: flowchart LR on the first line, then one line per box and the arrow into it; click again (it is now “Show diagram”) to go back. When a box says something wrong, the source tells you whether the assistant wrote it wrong or it was drawn wrong.

Besides flowcharts it can draw timelines, pie charts, mind maps, sequence diagrams and more, for example “draw the papers in the library by year of publication”. Every word in a diagram is the model's: the order of the steps and the numbers (here 65,691 cells or nuclei, 32 clusters) need checking against the original, just like the quotes of step 3.

Recap

  • The scope is the list currently shown in “All papers”; you can search first, or narrow it or read just one paper in the “Papers” panel.
  • After you ask, the “Provided…” line and the “Papers” panel show what was handed to the model this time.
  • Sources with a page number and a quoted sentence are compared word for word, the result is written on the card, and clicking the card goes back to that page of the PDF.
  • “Exact quote found” says only that the words are in the original; whether the conclusion holds is for /verify and your own reading of the context to decide.
  • What the library cannot answer, the assistant can search the web for (“Settings › Network › Web search”); what leaves is the search words, the service bills for it, and web pages get no word-for-word check, so open them yourself.
  • Ask the assistant to draw a diagram and it writes Mermaid, which the window draws; over the diagram are “Actual size”, “Copy image” and “Show source”.
  • To read the whole library into one table, paper by paper, continue with Read a batch of papers into one table; for a deep read of one paper, go back to Read one paper closely.

About this tutorial. The screenshots were retaken in November 2026 on macOS with v0.18.0, with the English interface and a stand-in model that answers by fixed rules, so the numbers in this tutorial are those of that run. A real model answers differently each time, so your wording and numbers will differ; the steps are the same. Steps 7 and 8 (Figures 7 to 12) come from another run with v0.18.0 on a library of 40 papers, with a real model, Claude Sonnet 5.5 (claude/claude-sonnet-5-5) called through a local gateway, and web search through DeepSeek; their questions were asked in English.

The assistant can be wrong. Confirm its suggestions and answers yourself; check key numbers and conclusions against the original.
