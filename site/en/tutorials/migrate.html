Tutorial 03 · Collect

Bring in the papers you already have: Zotero, PDFs and records

The worst part of switching tools is that everything is scattered. Some of it is in Zotero, some is PDFs you saved over the years, and some is a .bib from your advisor and a few DOIs noted in a report. This tutorial moves all of it into one library, keeps one copy of each duplicate, completes the records that were not recognized and finds the missing full texts.

Scenario
The project has been running for a while. The papers are spread over Zotero, a PDF folder with subfolders by species, a .bib and a few DOIs.
Needs
A library (it can be empty) and a computer that is online. No model is needed: no step here goes through a model provider.
Time
About 10 minutes, of which a few minutes are waiting for database lookups and full-text downloads.

1Ways to add papers

This example starts from an empty library, “Plant single-cell transcriptomics”. Every way in is under “Add papers” at the top left:

  • “Add by DOI / PMID…”: one paper at a time, with the shortcut ⌘ + N (Ctrl + N on Windows).
  • “Import PDF files…” and “Import folder…”: the text is read, a DOI is looked for on the first two pages and the record is looked up. The names of subfolders become tags.
  • “Import BibTeX / RIS…”: records only, without PDFs.
  • “Import from Zotero…”: reads the Zotero database on this computer and brings records, tags, notes and PDFs together.

However many times you move things, no paper appears twice. Each incoming paper is matched by DOI first, then by PMID, and last by title. A paper already in the library keeps its record untouched. Papers in the trash count as already there: one you deleted does not come back because you imported it again.

2Move over from Zotero

Start with Zotero, because its records are the most complete and the tags and notes are there too.

  1. In Zotero, wait for the sync to finish. Open each PDF you want to take along once, to make sure it has been downloaded to this computer, then quit Zotero. If you only close the window, it may still hold the database in the background.
  2. In Wisp Study, click “Add papers › Import from Zotero…”, read the note, click “Choose zotero.sqlite…” and pick zotero.sqlite in the Zotero data folder. You can open that folder from Zotero's Settings › Advanced › Files and Folders.

If you have joined groups in Zotero besides “My Library”, the database holds more than one library. Since v0.17.0, once you have picked zotero.sqlite you are first asked which one to import. Each row is a library with its number of items (not counting notes, attachments and what is in Zotero's trash). The last row, “All”, combines them. “My Library” is selected by default. Pick one and click “Import”. When only one library has items, this step is skipped and the import starts at once.

The import opens the Zotero database read-only. PDFs are copied, the original files are not touched, and this step does not go online. The Zotero library in this example is small, 6 papers, and the import finished in a few seconds:

This time the result was “6 added · 0 existing · 0 in trash · 0 failed · 4 PDFs added · 1 pending”, and one web snapshot was not moved. Look at the tags on the left. Zotero's tags came over as they were (“精读” (close reading) and “数据库” (database)). Collections became tags with a path, such as Zotero/方法/注释 (Zotero/Methods/Annotation), and one click filters that collection. Notes attached to a paper were merged into the paper's note, and the PMID in Extra was recognized too.

The pending item is the Multiome paper: Zotero has an attachment record for it, but the file was never downloaded. The record came in anyway, and its full text is left for step 6.

For what is moved, common errors (such as “the database is in use”) and how to move only part of it, see Importing papers from Zotero (in Chinese).

TipOne paper in Zotero, about dorsal root ganglia, was saved by mistake long ago and has nothing to do with plants. Click “Move to trash” in its details. The folder in the next step also holds its PDF, so you will see what happens to it.

3Import a whole folder of PDFs

The PDFs collected over the years sit in one folder, split into subfolders: “rice”, “arabidopsis” and “methods”. This example has 6 files, 5 of them in subfolders and one, myrosin-cells.pdf, in none. File names can be anything.

Click “Add papers › Import folder…” and choose this folder, or drag it straight into the window. The import runs in the background. A progress block, “Import PDFs”, with a count and a segmented bar under it, appears at the bottom of the sidebar, and it stays when you switch to another page. Each file has its text read, its DOI looked for and its record looked up once more, which can take a while. When it ends, the block turns into “PDF import complete 6/6”. Click it to open the details:

  • Added 5: the 4 papers with a recognized DOI came in with complete records. arabidopsis-root-hairs.pdf had no DOI on its first pages, so its entry was made from the file name and is marked for a second look. Step 5 completes it.
  • Attached to existing paper: this run had none. A PDF that is the same file as one already stored, or whose DOI matches a paper that has no file yet, is attached to that paper instead of becoming a second one.
  • In trash, not imported 1: myrosin-cells.pdf is a paper that was moved to the trash before this import, and it did not come back.

The Tags column on the left now has “arabidopsis” (2 papers), “methods” (2) and “rice” (1). A PDF that is in none of the subfolders gets no tag.

4The advisor's .bib and the DOIs in a report

The advisor's 导师推荐.bib (advisor's picks) has 6 entries. Click “Add papers › Import BibTeX / RIS…” and choose it, or drag it into the window. The top of the window says “Imported 5 papers, skipped 1 existing.” The skipped one is NRTPredictor, which Zotero already had. Papers from a .bib are records only, and their Full text column says “Linked”.

Two papers were noted down in a report, one with a DOI and one with only a PMID. Click “Add papers › Add by DOI / PMID…” and paste:

Click “Look up and add”. A few seconds later the maize root paper comes in, with title, authors, journal and abstract filled in. Open the dialog again and enter the PMID 42021342. The paper that arrives is the reed invasion one (Genome Biology, 2026). You can also paste a doi.org link. Papers that came in as records only, like these, are listed in the “Missing PDF” view.

5Complete a PDF that was not recognized, by title

The list's “Full text” column has four states: “No full text”, “Linked” (there is a DOI or a web address but no file), “PDF saved”, and “PDF without text” (a file is saved but no text could be read from it, so for now it cannot be used to ask questions). The “Full text” filter above the table header shows just one of them.

What needs work now is arabidopsis-root-hairs.pdf from step 3. It is “PDF saved”, yet its title is still the file name, and the authors and year are missing, because no DOI was found on its first pages. The same steps complete any record that has little more than a title, such as a paper you entered by hand with “Add papers › Enter manually…”. The figures below use such a paper: it was entered with only the title “Evolutionary diversity of cell-type-specific expression and stress response in Brassicaceae roots”.

  1. In the details, click “Look up by title”.
  2. The box holds the title to search for. When the paper's title is only a file name, the title is read from the PDF's first page and a note asks you to check it before searching. For arabidopsis-root-hairs.pdf it reads “Mapping gene activity of Arabidopsis root hairs”. If no title can be read, the box is empty and you type it. Authors and year may stay empty.
  3. Click “Search”.

Here Crossref returned 5 results, Europe PMC 2 and arXiv none. They merged into two candidates: the 2024 preprint and the 2026 published version in Nature Communications, both marked as a title match. Being listed first only means the title is closer. For arabidopsis-root-hairs.pdf the same lookup found a single record, the 2013 article in Genome Biology. This example takes the published version. Click it to check it field by field:

All the fields of this paper are empty, so all are ticked by default. Click “Save 10 fields”. A message says “Completed” with the title, and the row now has authors, a year and the journal.

NoteOnly the title, authors and year are sent in the lookup. The PDF is not uploaded, and no model is involved. A similar title does not mean the same paper: when unsure, click “Open web page” to compare. A candidate that is already another paper in the library cannot be written into this one. If nothing turns up, fill it in by hand with “Edit information and fields”. A paper may stay without a DOI forever.

6Find the missing full texts

Records that came from a .bib, a DOI or Zotero without a file have no PDF. Click “Missing PDF” on the left to list them. The figure shows a library in which 12 papers are in that state:

It looks only for versions that can be had free and legally: arXiv, the open access files listed by OpenAlex and Europe PMC, and the PDFs at the publisher for Springer / BMC, Nature and Frontiers articles that they mark as open access. It does not sign in and does not get around paywalls. A paper with no DOI, PMID or arXiv ID cannot be looked up.

Click “Find open full text for 12 papers”. Another progress block, “Find full text”, appears at the bottom of the sidebar. Its bar is segmented by color: green is saved, yellow is no URL found, red is download failed, blue is being searched and gray is waiting. Click it to open “Full text search”, where you see which papers are being looked up and the result and reason for each. While it runs you can “Stop searching”, or “Collapse” it and do something else. When it finishes, the block says “Search complete”:

The 8 saved are the three Frontiers in Plant Science papers, the Nature Communications paper, the two BMC Plant Biology papers, the Genome Biology paper and the Plant Cell paper. Their PDFs were attached to the existing entries. Of the other 4, two are open access articles whose addresses were found but not served to a program: one from MDPI returned HTTP 403, and the other returned a sign-in or verification page, which was not stored as a PDF. For the last two, the New Phytologist and Plant Physiology papers, no download address was found. “Missing PDF” still holds 4 papers.

For the rest, click “Open web page” in the details, download the PDF in your browser, then drag the file into the Wisp Study window. It reads the DOI, recognizes that the library already has this paper and attaches the PDF to the existing entry. The details say “Paper already in library; PDF attached to existing record”, and no extra record appears. The “PDF” tab of a single paper also has two buttons, “Find open full text” and “Add PDF”.

TipA failed download does not mean the paper has no open full text. It only means it could not be fetched for you this time. Try again later in “Missing PDF”: the button only looks for papers that still have no PDF.

Recap

  • From Zotero came the records of 6 papers with their tags, collections, notes and 4 PDFs. Zotero itself was not changed.
  • A folder of 6 PDFs was imported and its subfolders became tags. 5 were added, one of them with no DOI detected, and the paper in the trash did not come back.
  • The .bib brought in 5 papers and skipped 1 existing one. One paper each was added by DOI and by PMID.
  • A record with only a title was completed by title into the published version in Nature Communications, not the preprint. Of 12 papers missing full text, 8 were found, and the reasons for the others are in the details.
  • No model was used anywhere. Next you can read one of these papers closely, or go back to start from a topic and fill out the library with a search.
