Tutorial 05 · Read

Read one paper closely: read, mark, ask, note

Next week it is your turn to present a paper at the lab meeting, so you need to understand this one thoroughly. This tutorial reads it from start to finish in Wisp Study: the abstract and the hard paragraphs next to a translation, highlights and underlines as you go, questions to the assistant about this paper wherever you are stuck with a check of the sentences it quotes, good answers saved as notes, and the figures you will present cut into the Figures page. The last two sections go back to the same paper, to try clicking a figure number in the text to see the figure, finding the text again from a figure, and writing text boxes on the page.

Scenario
Close reading of a paper on cell-type-specific immune programs in the Arabidopsis leaf epidermis, in preparation for a lab meeting.
Needs
A paper with its PDF, and a model provider set up in “Settings › AI”.
Time
About 15 minutes, not counting reading the paper itself.

Contents

1Open the paper and read the translated abstract first

The paper to read is Cell-type-specific immune programs orchestrate spatial defense in the Arabidopsis leaf epidermis, published in Nature Communications in 2026. It has 17 pages, and the PDF is already stored in the library “Plant single-cell transcriptomics”. Type Cell-type-specific immune programs in the search box above the list and click this paper. The details open on the right. They have three tabs: “Info” holds the record, status, abstract and notes, “PDF” is the full text, and “Ask” is a conversation about this paper only.

In “Info”, click “Show translation” at the right of the abstract heading. The title and abstract are replaced in place by the translation. The target language comes from the translation prompt in “Settings › AI”: for the pictures in this tutorial it was set to Spanish, so the translation reads in Spanish. The button at the same spot above the list becomes “Show original”, and one more click switches back.

The translation is made by the model you assign to translation in “Settings › AI”, and the title and abstract are sent to that model provider. Translations are saved in the library, so the same text is not requested twice. The original is the authority for terms and numbers.

2Go into reading mode

Switch to “PDF”. The page opens at “Fit width”, and the zoom in the toolbar reads something like “Fit width · 52%”. The − and + on either side zoom out and in, and the zoom value itself is a menu with “Fit width”, “Fit page” and a few percentages. After you zoom in by hand to see a figure, “Fit width” appears next to the zoom, and one click takes you back.

The details panel is too narrow for close reading. Click the expand icon at the right of the title at the top of the details (the hover tip says “Reading mode: the whole window for this paper”, shortcut ⌘ + Shift + F, or Ctrl + Shift + F on Windows). The sidebar and the paper list fold away and the whole window is left to this paper. You can click it on the “Info” page too, without switching to “PDF” first. Only the title and the three tabs remain at the top, and the icon at the same place becomes a restore icon (the hover tip says “Leave reading mode Esc”). Click it again or press Esc to leave. In this example, fit width in reading mode is 169% in the window, and the text is about as large as on paper.

On page 2, in the opening paragraph, the authors say what single-cell and spatial transcriptomics have changed and what is still missing. Drag to select its last two sentences, from “In contrast, recent advances…” to “…an urgent and unresolved challenge.”. When you release the mouse, a row of buttons appears beside the selection: “Copy”, “Underline”, “Highlight”, “Add to chat”, “Translate”, “Add to map” and “Read aloud”. Click “Translate” and the translation appears in a card beside the selection, with “Copy translation” on it.

TipBefore v0.17.0 the top right of the window had a text button in reading mode, “× Leave reading Esc”. From v0.17.0 it is a restore icon, as in the pictures here, and it is used the same way.

What came out this time was the Spanish text in the card, beginning “En cambio, los recientes avances en transcriptómica unicelular y espacial han comenzado a revelar estados celulares inmunitarios distintos…”. Superscript reference numbers in the text are translated along with it as plain text (“inmunidad vegetal3–6”), so keep that in mind as you read.

Next to a translation: each page beside its translation

If you want to read the whole paper in translation first and then go back to the original, click “Translation” in the toolbar. The page splits into two halves. On the left is the original. On the right is the same page with each paragraph replaced by its translation, while layout, figures, tables and page numbers stay in place, and the two halves scroll together. Translation proceeds from the page you are looking at onward, several paragraphs at a time, and each paragraph shows as soon as it is done. The button shows the progress. In this example, a few seconds in, it read “16 / 92 paragraphs”, with “Stop” beside it.

Each paragraph is saved in the library as soon as it is translated. If you stop in the middle or close it, “Continue translating” next time translates only what is left. What has been translated shows at once when you open it again, and you can read it offline. With “Translation” on, switching to another paper first shows only the translations that exist, and “Translate full text” starts the work. How many paragraphs are translated at the same time is set under “Settings › AI › Tasks” in “Concurrent translations”. When the window is narrow the two halves are stacked, and choosing “Single view” in “Split” folds the translation away. A scanned file needs a text layer first.

Split: the figure on one side, the text on the other

The text on page 2 refers to Fig. 1 again and again, and Fig. 1 is on page 3. Click “Split” in the toolbar. The menu has “Single view”, “Side by side”, “Stacked” and “Free: drag the line between the halves, zoom each on its own”, and when the paper has attachments, below them “Other half: Body · …” and “Other half: Attachment S1 · …”, to open another file in the other half. Choose “Side by side”: the same PDF is split into two halves that scroll on their own. With one half on the text and the other on the figure, you do not have to flip back and forth. In “Side by side” the halves are even and zoom together, and selecting text, annotating, screenshots and translation work in either half. Choose “Free” if you want to drag the line between the halves and zoom each half on its own. Choose “Single view” to fold the split away. A split belongs to the current paper only. It goes back to a single view when you switch papers.

3Highlight, underline and write comments

The sentence that names the open question, “Despite these advances, fully elucidating the temporal dynamics of immune activation… remains an urgent and unresolved challenge.”, is the one to open the lab meeting with. Select it and click “Highlight”. A new highlight is yellow. If you want colors by purpose, click the highlight once and a small menu appears beside it with seven color dots, yellow, green, blue, pink, purple, red and orange, the current one ringed, and “Remove highlight”, which removes just that one. In this example it was changed to green. For the last sentence of the next paragraph, “Despite these insights, the mechanisms underlying GC-autonomous immunity remain poorly understood.”, select it and click “Underline”. Dragging over a highlight still selects text, so it does not change a color by accident.

“Annotations (2)” at the top right of the page unfolds all the annotations of this PDF. Each shows its page number and type, and you can “Locate” it in the page, write a “Note” and “Delete” it. Write a reminder to yourself on the highlight, as the note “Say this gap first.” in the picture, and click “Save note”.

TipAnnotations are stored in the library's database and drawn over the PDF by page coordinates. The original PDF file is not changed by a single byte. So another reader on your system will not show these highlights when it opens the original, and in return you need not worry about messing up the publisher's file.

4Ask about this paper and check the sources

The methods need to be clear, so ask the assistant first. Click “Ask”. The window changes to the ask layout: the sidebar folds away, the papers become a narrow list at the left (↑↓ switches paper), the conversation is in the middle, and this paper's PDF is on the right. Above the conversation a line (cut off at this width) says what will be provided: this paper's record and its 17 full-text pages, which is what will be sent to the model provider you set up. The empty conversation lists three common questions, and one click sends one. Here you write your own:

Ask

How was calcium influx and ROS production measured in guard cells? Please cite page numbers.

The assistant in these pictures is a stand-in script, so its answer is plain: three bullets, each a page number and a sentence quoted from that page (pages 1, 2 and 8), chosen because they share the most words with the question. A real model writes a fuller answer, with tables where they help, and says so when the full text does not report something.

The green [P11 p.8 "…"] in the answer is a source: a page number plus a sentence of the original. Under the answer, each source is a card, and on the right end of a card with a quotation is the result of the check. “Exact quote found” means the sentence really is on that page. Other results are “Quote found on p.N” (on a different page), “Only the first N% matches” or “Quote absent from source”. Click a card and the PDF on the right jumps to that page and marks the sentence.

Note“Exact quote found” answers “is the quoted sentence in the original”, not “is the conclusion of the answer right”. The assistant can still misread a sentence when it puts it into its own explanation, so open the card for a key number and check the context. Under the conversation it always says “Answers may be wrong; check the original”.

Follow up with a passage of the original

Suppose you doubt one sentence in the abstract, for example the one saying that GCs fail to activate SA biosynthesis or SA-responsive genes. On the PDF at the right, select this sentence and click “Add to chat” among the buttons beside the selection. It appears above the input box as “Original · Page 1” and is handed to the model as a quotation when you ask. Then ask:

Ask

Is the evidence for this statement sufficient? Which experiments support it, and what limitations do the authors admit themselves?

A capable model may first say what it will check and then go through the full text itself with its tools, and a line such as “Reading full text” appears in the conversation while it does. What it answers depends on the model, and the stand-in used for these pictures makes no such judgment, so there is no picture of this step. Whatever the answer says about the evidence, replicates or limitations, it is worth discussing at the lab meeting. Before you present, turn to the pages it cited and look yourself.

5Screenshots: ask about a figure, save it to Figures

You will present Fig. 1 at the lab meeting, so first make sure you understand it. In the PDF on the right, go to page 3 and click “Screenshot” in the toolbar (the scissors icon, which turns green and becomes “Cancel screenshot”), then frame the whole figure on the page. Once framed, the box is green, and below it is a row of “Save to figures”, “Ask about screenshot”, “Mark” and “Cancel”. “Mark” leaves this region on the page as a dashed box, so you can save or ask about it later.

Click “Ask about screenshot” and the screenshot is added above the input box as an image. The image is sent to the model provider, so the model you set up must be able to see images. Ask:

Ask

The screenshot is Fig. 1. What do panels A to D show, and which time points are compared?

A model that can see images answers from the figure and from the text. The stand-in used for these pictures cannot, so there is no picture of this step. Whatever a model reads off a figure, check it against the legend of Fig. 1, which is on page 4.

Save it to Figures

Frame Fig. 1 again, and this time click “Save to figures”. The dialog that opens has the screenshot on the left and four fields on the right: figure label, caption, tags and notes. The label and caption are usually read from the text of this page, but in this paper the caption is on the next page, and the dialog says “No nearby caption found on this page. Enter one or leave it blank.” Click the AI icon at the right of “Caption”, and a model that can see images writes a caption from the figure. What it writes stays in the field, and you can edit it. The “Figure label” was still empty this time, so fill in Fig. 1 yourself. The stand-in model used for these pictures cannot look at images, so the AI icons were not used: the caption was filled in by hand from the caption on page 4. Only “Save figure” stores it.

The one above is how it looks in v0.17.0. The PDF is no longer dimmed while the dialog is open, and the page goes back to text selection, so when a caption is elsewhere you can select and copy it right on the page and paste it into “Caption”. Hold the title of the dialog or the picture on its left and you can drag it aside, and it stays there the next time you save one.

After you click “Save figure”, a green dashed outline stays around the figure on the PDF, with a check mark and the figure label at the top left, so on whatever page you are, you can see which figures are already saved. Click this label and the dialog changes to “Figures” beside the page. You can edit the label, caption, tags and notes, drag the four corners of the box on the page to frame again, and click “Save”. “Remove” undoes the saving.

Saved figures are under “Favorites” on the left, with “Figures” selected at the top. A card shows the thumbnail, the source, the page and the caption, and you can search captions, tags, notes and the paper they belong to. Open a figure: “Open source” opens this paper's PDF on the right, stopped at page 3 where the screenshot was taken, and “Ask about image” takes it back into this paper's conversation.

6Save as a note and set the reading status

A good answer is worth keeping, such as the one in section 4. Under the answer there is “Save as note”. It opens an editable window that says it will “append to “My notes” ..., and existing notes are not touched”. The note is provided to later questions together with the paper's record, so first correct what is wrong, then click “Save note”. What is saved starts with “问：” (Q:) and the question. The citations lose the P11 numbering, which only means something within this conversation, and keep the page number and the original sentence. At the end a line is added automatically: “（日期 提问所得 · 模型的回答，未核对原文）” (the date of the question, then: asked · the model's answer, not checked against the original).

Finally go back to “Info”, click “Unread” in the status row and choose “Read closely”. In the list, the reading column of this paper becomes “Read closely”, and the “Unread” count on the left goes down by one. Below is “My notes”, with the answer you just saved in it. “Save / Export” at the right of the heading can save the notes, together with chosen questions and answers, to Notion or to a local file.

TipThe conversation itself is kept too: “History” at the top of the “Ask” page lists every conversation held about this paper, and a click on a row carries on asking. Highlights, notes, figures and conversations are all stored in this library's folder.

7See figures as you read: click a figure number in the text

In section 2, to compare against Fig. 1, you opened a split and went to the page of the figure yourself. From v0.17.0 the figure number in the text can be clicked itself. This paper is a good fit: Fig. 1 is on page 3 but its caption is on page 4, and its supplementary figures are not in this PDF. No supplementary file is attached to the paper in the pictures of this section, except for Figure 15, which comes from an earlier run in which a paper's supplementary material (Supplementary Information) was downloaded from the publisher's site and attached to it as Attachment S1.

Go into reading mode and turn to page 2, to the paragraph of the results that begins “Leaves inoculated with B. hordei were sampled at three time points”. A figure number such as Fig. 1A, B or Fig. 1C, D turns the pointer into a hand and gets an underline when you move over it. Click Fig. 1A, B in this paragraph, and a card opens beside the number. Its title row reads “Fig. 1” and “Figure on page 3, caption on page 4”. Below is the whole of Fig. 1, and scrolling down shows the caption from page 4. The page stays on page 2. Press Esc, click × or click elsewhere on the page to fold the card away.

The card has three buttons at the bottom. “Related text” on the left is covered later, and on the right are “Pin beside” and “Go to figure”.

Pin beside

Click “Pin beside”. The reading area splits into two halves: the left is still the text of page 2, and the right stops at Fig. 1 on page 3. The split icon in the toolbar turns green, and the zoom becomes the fit width of each half (“Fit width · 82%” here). Keep reading and the figure stays beside you. When you are done, choose “Single view” in “Split”.

Go to figure, and come back

To look at the figure carefully, click “Go to figure”. The page jumps to Fig. 1 on page 3, and “Back to where you were” appears at the bottom left with “Page 2” beside it. When you have looked, click it to return to the page, the spot and the zoom you had before the jump. The × at its right only folds the prompt away.

From the figure to the text: Related text

It works the other way too. “Related text” at the bottom left of the card first shows the caption. Below it is “4 places in the text speak of it”, each with the section and the page, such as “Results · Page 2” (the file is named too when the paper has attachments), followed by a passage starting at the sentence that mentions it. The first three places for Fig. 1 are on pages 2, 3 and 4, and the fourth is further down the card. Click a row and the page jumps to that passage, with the figure number marked in yellow, and “Back to where you were” appears at the bottom left again. The small icon at the right of a row pins that passage in the other half, and the ‹ at the left of the title goes back to the figure. The figure number at the start of the caption can be clicked too, and it opens this list directly.

It finds only paragraphs in which the figure number is written. A paragraph that discusses the figure without writing its number is not in the list. The section is recognized from headings that stand on a line of their own, so for a manuscript where the heading and the text share a line, it is not written.

Between the text and the attachment

The next picture is from the earlier run, in the Chinese interface, with another paper that had a PDF attachment; the paper in this tutorial has none. When the paper has a PDF attachment, a figure number can point into the attachment too. Take the same sentence on page 2 and click the 1 in Supplementary Figs. 1, 2. The card title reads “Supplementary Fig. 1” and “Attachment S1 · Page 1”, and it shows the schematic of the ligular region's morphology and cells from the supplementary material. The 2 on its own after it opens Supplementary Fig. 2. Now “Pin beside” puts the attachment in the other half. “Go to figure” switches to the attachment first, the file name in the toolbar becomes “Attachment S1 · Supplementary Information.pdf”, and the bottom left reads “Back to where you were Body · Page 2”, which switches back to the text when clicked. When a figure number you read in the attachment exists in both the text and the attachment, the card first says “2 figures it may mean”, each row giving the figure number, the file, the page and the start of the caption, and you pick a row to look at.

TipYou download the supplementary material from the publisher's site yourself, then add it to the paper as an attachment. Figures are matched by number: when a caption in the attachment reads Supplementary Fig. 1 or Figure S1, a Supplementary Fig. 1 or Fig. S1 in the text looks for it. Where no file has an S1 and the attachment's figure is written only Figure 1, it is also matched by number. If the wrong file is attached, the figure that opens will not match the text, so check the caption before you rely on it.
TipA PDF does not say where its figures are. The extent of a figure is estimated from the caption and from what is drawn on the page, and some journal layouts get framed inaccurately. When no figure is found, the card shows only the caption. After a file is opened it has to be read once in the background to know where each figure is, so for a long file or an attachment the figure numbers can be clicked only after a second or two. A scanned file has no text layer, and this does not apply.

8Write on the page: text boxes and boxes

Highlights and underlines are marked on the original text. To write a sentence of your own in the blank space of a page, use “Text box” in the toolbar. Click it, drag out a box on the page, and when you let go you can type. In this example, a sentence to say first at the lab meeting was written in the blank space to the left of the abstract on page 1. The text wraps by itself inside the box, and the box grows taller with the text.

When you have written it, click elsewhere on the page, then click this text box once and it is selected: eight handles appear at the corners and the midpoints of the sides. Dragging anywhere inside the box moves it, and dragging a handle resizes it. The small menu below has three colors, “Fill”, “Border” and “Text”, plus “Edit text” and “Delete text box”.

“Box” is drawn the same way: drag out a frame around a passage. Boxes used to be movable only, with no resizing. Now click the edge of a box to select it, and it has eight handles too. Dragging the four corners changes the width and height together, dragging the middle of a side pulls only the width or only the height, and dragging the edge moves it. In this example a box was first drawn around one line of the abstract, “display rapid calcium influx and pronounced reactive oxygen species bursts,”, then the handle at the lower right was pulled down one line so that the box also encloses “transmitting immune signals to adjacent pavement cells via the apoplast.”. When selected, it shows seven colors and “Delete box” below.

Text boxes and boxes are stored in the library like highlights and drawn over the page, and the PDF file itself is not changed. They are in the “Annotations” list at the top right too.

Recap

  • You went through an English paper with “Show translation”, “Translate” on a selected passage and “Translation” for the whole page (in Spanish here), reading side by side in reading mode and in a split.
  • Highlights, color changes and underlines, with notes written in the “Annotations” list. None of this changes the PDF file itself.
  • You asked about this paper, jumped back to the original sentence by a source card and “Exact quote found” to check it, and followed up with a selected passage of the original or a screenshot.
  • Fig. 1 went into Figures with its label, caption and tag. A saved figure keeps a green dashed outline on the page. A good answer can be saved as a note, and the status set to “Read closely”.
  • Clicking a figure number in the text shows the figure and its caption. “Pin beside” puts it in the other half, and after “Go to figure” you use “Back to where you were” to return. A supplementary figure number points to the supplementary material attached to this paper, and “Related text” shows which paragraphs of the text mention a figure.
  • “Text box” writes on the page, and text boxes and boxes both have eight handles for resizing.
  • Next: widen the questions to the whole library, see Ask the whole library, and check the answers. To read the key information of a batch of papers into a table, see Read a batch of papers into one table.
